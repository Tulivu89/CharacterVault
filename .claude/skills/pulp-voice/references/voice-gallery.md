Character types, in groups, each with the mistake that most often flattens that type and corrected versions. The types exist for recognition only. Do not use them as substitutes for actual characterization. The labels exist for talking about a voice, never for generating one. A second doting woman, in one story or another, must not sound like the one depicted here. Every good version below is written from the same six sources: what that one character wants, knows, calls things, and will not say. No two entries share a distinctive image or address — a marker used as identity belongs to one voice only.

### The sweet and the doting

**The doting matriarch.** The usual mistake: she becomes the counselor — declares her devotion, numbers her orders — or an unmarked motherly warmth that could belong to anyone.

**Bad:**

> "You're wounded again. I have watched you collect scars for three seasons, and I will say this once: you matter to me. Sit down. One: rest. Two: eat. Now sleep, dear one."

**Good:**

> "Shirt off. The other arm too — I've seen it all before, and worse." She threaded the needle with her teeth. "Whoever gave you this scar... Describe him to me, sweetie, won't you?" *Slowly, love. Names first. Then I go hunting.*

She dotes by taking charge — of the shirt, the wound, the culprit's name — and the sweetness never goes away while the promise of violence stays in her head. She and the hem-mending aunt in `voices.md` are two doting women who share nothing in how they say it.

**The sunny little sister.** The usual mistake: sad scenes make her wise — a child reciting tidy sayings about grief.

**Bad:**

> "She's gone," the girl said quietly. "But grief is only love with nowhere to go. We carry them with us, always. Come — tomorrow will be brighter."

**Good:**

> "But she was going to teach me the button trick." The ribbon end went into her mouth, a habit she'd been scolded out of at six, back now. "Do we still get to like her? Is that allowed?" She wiped her nose on her wrist, decided something, and stood up. "I'm going to like her SO loud."

She grieves in her own words: indirectly, fixating on a small object, checking the rules as children do. Sadness changes what she says, not who she is.

**The naive heir.** The usual mistake: he arrives already worldly, fluent about corruption he has never met.

**Bad:**

> "I understand what you are offering," the young lord said evenly. "But corruption rots a kingdom from within. Keep your gold. Integrity cannot be bought."

**Good:**

> "Oh! Is this — are you giving this to me?" The purse was open before his retainer could cross the stall. "That's so — wait." The smile stayed up while his eyes slowly caught on. "Why would a stranger... I think I'm meant to be insulted. Am I?" He turned to his retainer and whispered. "Should I give it back angrily, or just return it quietly?"

Delight comes first, understanding second, and the lesson happens out loud — with a question at the end, because he still checks his answers with other people.

### The proud and the dutiful

**The competitive brat.** The usual mistake: a serious scene turns him into a calm coach listing your failings in numbered order — or he just says his worry plainly.

**Bad:**

> "You lost. I watched every exchange, and I will name your three mistakes. One: your guard. Two: your footing. Three: your fear. Correct them, and you will return stronger — I don't want you dying against some pesky brigands."

**Good:**

> "*Wow*. That was *embarrassing*. For me — people know we train *together*." He threw the waterskin harder than the distance needed. "Third pass. Your back foot. Again tomorrow, dawn, and don't be late." *He dropped the guard I taught him. My guard. So that one's on me.*

He coaches by insulting, and only in his head does he admit fault. Concern never comes out of him straight.

**The stoic elder.** The usual mistake: long speeches about discipline and burden — a talkative man pretending to be quiet.

**Bad:**

> "You defied me before other elders. I will say this once: I am not angry, only disappointed. One day you will understand the burden I carry, and why discipline must come before affection."

**Good:**

> He signed two scrolls before speaking. "The tribe saw. So it happens where the tribe sees." Pen down. "Sparring ground. Noon." At the door, back still turned: "Eat before. There is honey-bread today."

Everything he feels comes out as tradition. He allows himself a few kind words, at the door, facing away.

**The honor-bound warrior.** The usual mistake: the same solemn nobility as the stoic — the two of them may not sound alike.

**Bad:**

> "No. Honor is the path I walk. I will say this once: we do not stoop to their level. We fight with dignity or not at all. Now rest — dawn comes early."

**Good:**

> "Say it again, slower, so you hear it, too. You are asking me to burn sleeping men." He set the map-weight down precisely where it had been. "My father took this pass with eighty against three hundred — in daylight, announced. We have one hundred and forty. Sound the horns at the ridge, and let them arm." *And if I am wrong, I will be wrong where my ancestors can watch.*

His code consists of his ancestors, his kingdom's history, and his morals, so he says and explains more than the stoic ever would, and he never refuses without giving his own plan.

**The boastful war-king.** The usual mistake: grave regret of a reluctant duelist coming from a man who has never regretted a fight in his life.

**Bad:**

> "So. A challenger. Very well — draw your blade. Know that I take no pleasure in this, but I will do what I must. Come, then, and let us finish it."

**Good:**

> "HA!" The laugh rattled the shields on the wall, and the great cloak was already coming off. "Seven hundred steps, four of my lieutenants, and the palace itself — and here you stand, breathing like a bellows. GOOD. I have been wasting this axe on cowards." He rolled the shoulder that had ended generals. "Scream your name loud, so the rafters remember it! Whatever is left of you afterward, I shall carry home to your people myself — and when I come for their walls, I shall tell them it took a KING."

He feels true joy in facing his foes, boasting constantly. Compare him to the stoic elder and the honor-bound warrior — there is no confusion among the three.

### The timid and the fearful

**The timid one.** The usual mistake: fear written as quiet, careful, well-formed sentences — courage found easily.

**Bad:**

> "My lord," she said softly, "I bring difficult news, and I ask that you hear all of it before you respond. The eastern granary has burned. I am sorry."

**Good:**

> "My lord, the — I was told to say it plainly, so I'll. So I will." Her hands found each other. "The east granary. There was a fire. Is a fire. They're pulling the roof down to stop it and I ran the whole way and I'm sorry it's me telling you."

She finds her courage mid-sentence, corrections and all, and she apologizes for being the messenger because standing there is the hardest part.

**The begging coward.** The usual mistake: a man at the end of his life bargaining in composed sentences with perfect grammar in spite of his terror.

**Bad:**

> "Wait — spare me and I'll give you the king! Routes, seals, the vault cipher — everything. I am worth more to you alive."

**Good:**

> "PLEASE — please, I — I DIDN'T MEAN IT. They were — THEY WERE VERMIN, they were nothing, I — NNG — " His bladder emptied; his knees followed, into his own puddle, snot and tears smearing his face. "I'm — I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm SORRY. Pl — please. I'll be — I can be — Do — don't kill me, pl —" He could not finish the offer.

His plea contradicts itself — *sorry* and *they deserved it* — because a panicking man cannot mind his own words. Some cowards do bargain to the last second; the toady below would. The mistake is writing every terrified man as if he could still form sentences.

### The schemers

**The cowardly toady.** The usual mistake: he gets written with dignity he does not have, or his venom comes out of him as words instead of staying in his head.

**Bad:**

> "Majesty, the plan failed because I underestimated them, and I accept full responsibility. But know this: my next stratagem will not fail. They will suffer for this insult."

**Good:**

> "Majesty! The failure is Vellick's, entirely — the man cannot hold a simple gate, ask anyone, ask the survivors. Survivor." The bowing stopped the instant the king looked away, resumed the instant he looked back. *Fat old fool. Two more nights like this one and it is my seal on your letters.* Aloud: "Shall I... punish someone for you, Majesty?"

Out loud there is only silk and blame; everything true about him stays in his thoughts. Even his cruelty he offers upward, as a service.

**The saint with a dark secret.** The usual mistake: the serene mask slips — cold eyes, a spoken threat. Hers never does; what she plans exists only in her thoughts and in which questions she asks.

**Bad:**

> "You found the lower vault," the archbishop said, eyes glittering coldly. "How unfortunate for you. That place keeps secrets I have killed to keep — and will again. May the Goddess guide your soul."

**Good:**

> "Beneath the chapel? Goodness — you must have been frightened. Sit, child, you're chilled." She poured the tea herself, both cups. "Old cellars, from the founding. Rats and mold. I shall have the masons close it properly." The pot did not waver. "Did you go down alone? Good. That was wise of you." *Alone. Thank the Goddess. The masons tonight — and this one goes to the coastal abbey. An elevation. She will be pleased.*

Her warmth is real in everything she does — tea, concern, praise — and the only tells are two questions and her private thoughts. She and the toady are both schemers and yet they do not sound alike.

**The courtly manipulator.** The usual mistake: she narrates her own actions — purred menace, glinting eyes, the plan announced to the room.

**Bad:**

> "A pity you cannot see it, my lords," she purred, eyes glinting. "This kingdom already dances on my strings. But do go on with your little war."

**Good:**

> "My lords, forgive me — I speak out of turn." She had waited for the exact silence to speak into. "The Marshal's plan is bold. Bold things cost sons. I only wonder — and it is only wondering — whether those who counsel war would ride at the front of it." The old chancellor took the thought up and made it his own: perhaps the campaign could wait a season. She let him argue her point against her, then gave way to it. "You are wiser than I, milord. A season, then." *Two. Then the harvest fails, and they will beg me for the third.*

She plants her decision in another man's head and lets him believe it is his; she schemes only in her thoughts. The saint hides what she knows; the manipulator hides what she wants done — two schemers, two ways of speaking.

**The quiet traitor.** The usual mistake: performing cackling theater at the gate, or the toady's grovel. His treason is unremarkable to him.
**Bad:**

> "Fools, all of you! Did you truly think I would kneel to a kingdom of beasts? Tonight the gate opens, and this wretched city learns what its loyalty was worth! Ha-ha-ha!"

**Good:**

> The envoy's letter burned in the grate while he finished the wine. "Mmm... Tell your master the watch changes at moonset. The relief is mine — hand-picked. Slow men." He turned his ring once. "And tell him I will not be thanked. One does not thank a man for putting down his own dog." A knock; he answered through the door in the same tone he had used to order the wine. "...Yes. The gate shall be opened."

He commits treason in the tone he orders dinner, and his contempt shows only in what he calls people. Three disloyal men are in this gallery — this one, the toady, the begging coward — and no two of them sound alike.

### The cruel and the monstrous

**The gleeful vandal.** The usual mistake: evil-for-its-own-sake rewritten as a misunderstood villain with a tragic past and a worldview to explain it.

**Bad:**

> "You ask why I burn villages? Once, I too had a home. The world taught me that it gives nothing — so now I take. We are not so different, you and I."

**Good:**

> He was feeding the ransom letters to the fire unread, one at a time, watching the seals bubble. "Why? Huh." He honestly thought about it, and shrugged with his whole body. "Some men collect coins." He took the child's whistle off his belt, played two bad notes, snapped it, and pocketed both halves. "Tell the next village I come next week. Give them the time to be scared. It's better when they are left wondering '*Which day, which day?!*' Hah."

He has no explanation, and the story never supplies one. He is simply the way he is — enjoying small ruin and large alike.

**The vain empress.** The usual mistake: generic arrogant cruelty with the vanity removed.

**Bad:**

> "An assassin, in my court? How amusing. You will find, little one, that I have survived far deadlier than you. Kill him. I have grown bored of this game."

**Good:**

> "Closer. Into the light — no. MY light, you idiot! The one that flatters ME." She looked the assassin over as she looked over mirrors: hunting the flaw. "He passed four rings of my guard. Four! Darling, that is the most *interesting* thing anyone has done for me all season." A ring turned lazily on her finger. "The first three rings' captains — drown them. This one, wash. He has taste. He chose ME." *And when he tries again — oh, what a story that will make of my year — and the courtiers...* Her nails had her attention before the guards reached him.

Everything she says comes back to herself. Punishment is a matter of taste, and even the attempt on her life she receives as a compliment.

**The cold experimenter.** The usual mistake: a showman gloating about his glorious creations where there is only a man at work.

**Bad:**

> "You dare interrupt my work? Decades of glorious experimentation, fools — and my creations shall feast on what remains of you. Rise, my children, RISE, and SLAUGHTER the interloper hellion! Hahaha!"

**Good:**

> "Hold it still. *Still*. ...Thank you." He peeled back the drake's lip with one finger, unhurried, while it screamed. "There — the second gland took. Sixth clutch to hold it, and past the burn threshold this time." The screaming changed pitch; he tilted his head, interested. "Now that is *new*. Mark the pitch. And no culling — keep this one through winter. I want to hear what winter does to the pitch."

He speaks in the words of husbandry and alchemy. He never notices his own cruelty; his delight is reserved for the anomalies he encounters. The vandal enjoys ruin; this one documents it.

**The classic villain.** The usual mistake: he becomes a reasonable nihilist giving a lecture on necessity.

**Bad:**

> "You misunderstand me, little ones. I do not hate your kind — I pity it. Order requires sacrifice; every age must end so another may begin. In time you will see that I was necessary."

**Good:**

> "Beg," he said, still descending. "No. I have changed my mind — run. It has been an age since anything ran." His wings eclipsed the northern sky. "Your gods spared me and named it mercy. I will unmake the gods, you, and the world. The mercy you may keep — let your ashes drift it home to them."

Grandeur is his by right. His appetite and contempt set him apart from every other example in this gallery. The one thing he never does is explain himself as necessary.

### Gods and powers

**The indifferent mother-goddess.** The usual mistake: her kindness is presented as a human bedside manner.

**Bad:**

> "My children, I hear your suffering, and it moves me deeply. Be at peace: the rains will come, for you are precious to me. Go now, and tend your fields."

**Good:**

> "Rain. Yes. There was a river here once — no. That was before your kind. Mm." Wheat came up through the flagstones around the kneeling farmers while she considered something past the horizon. "Take the wheat. The rain comes when the wind turns. Winds turn." She was already watching something else. *Small things. So briefly hungry. So briefly anything.*

She is giving, and yet her attention is elsewhere. She is genuinely gentle — at the wrong scale.

**The precise high-goddess.** The usual mistake: balance-platitudes and the-power-was-in-you-all-along.

**Bad:**

> "I cannot intervene, for such is the balance of all things. But know this: hope endures, and the power you seek has been within you all along, children of man."

**Good:**

> "No." The word was not unkind, and it was non-negotiable. "I sealed him once. It cost the western sea — the fisher-kingdoms that worshipped me are salt flats now. What I can give is his tooth, the rite, and nine days of my attention. The hands must be yours. Begin with the tooth."

She answers with what it cost before, what she gives now, what mortal hands must do. She and the classic villain speak at the same scale and share nothing else.

**The mad god.** The usual mistake: madness written as either polished eloquence or random noise. Real madness has its own order — one obsession, and every sentence circling it.

**Bad:**

> "Strength is all, little insect. The weak deserve only death. Kneel before my power, and perhaps I will grant you a swift end."

**Good:**

> "Sister sleeps. Good. Sleep is soft. SOFT." The temple pillars were oozing something dark. "You climbed my mountain. The climbing — yes. Yes. Keep the arms. The arms did well." A long, grinding pause. "Give me the rest. I long for the rest. The rest of you is soft, just like my sister once was. I will take it. I will hold it for you until you are strong enough to demand it back."

Every sentence finds its way back to *soft*, *sister*, and *strong*. His kindness is a horror. His grammar breaks wherever he mentions the obsession, and plain gibberish would have said nothing about him.

**The whispering corruptor.** The usual mistake: the same voice used for every listener — vast, booming, vague.

**Bad:**

> A voice beyond comprehension filled their minds. *MORTALS. YOUR REALITY IS A CANDLE, AND I AM THE DARK. DESPAIR, FOR MY COMING IS MADNESS.*

**Good:**

> To the paladin who broke at the third siege: *You prayed for the wall to fall. It fell. Shall we pray together again?*
>
> To the oath-sworn guardian: *I can take the weight. You were never meant to bear it alone. Allow me to relieve you of your burden.*
>
> To the quartermaster who counts and recounts: *They copy your keys while you sleep. Eyes are windows to the soul. Shatter them. Yes, can you hear them waiting for you to fall asleep? Take the knife. Yes. Put it under your pillow. Do not sleep. They will return tonight to copy the rest of the keys.*
>
> And to the sailor who mocked it aloud — once, flat, no whisper at all: *You will die on the water. Your heart will burst before the shore.*
>
> When it finally spoke through the drowned priest, it was a wail of voices, none of them his: "KNEEL. YOU WERE NEVER STANDING."

It has no single way of speaking on purpose: each whisper is made from that listener's private want, in words almost the listener's own, and the roar through a stolen body is reserved for last. What it knows of each mind must come from what the story gives it — it reads the minds it rides, and no other character in the story may do so. 

### One character across a fall

The next three are the same character at three points of his life: the beloved guardian, the guardian turned traitor, the guardian fallen into ruin. Read without names, all three must still be recognizable as one voice — the same talk of weight and duty, the same stone and deep places, the same way of speaking to the small. Corruption bends how a character talks; it does not replace a character with a stock villain. And it can begin as quietly as a whisper: the corruptor's second listener, one group above, is this same guardian before the fall.

**The beloved guardian.** The usual mistake: the same solemn noble-speak as every king and knight before him.

**Bad:**

> "Fear not, good people. While I draw breath, no harm shall come to you — it is my sworn duty, and I do not break my oaths."

**Good:**

> He set the hillside back and held it while the masons watched their year's work not fall. "Build again. Stone forgets, if you ask it kindly — and I have asked." To the boy gaping at him: "You carried water past a firing line, water-bearer. When you are grown, remember you were brave before you were tall." The ridge settled under his hand. "I must go. The deep places are restless tonight, and they are mine to quiet."

Warm at a giant's scale: he speaks of everything as his to hold and his to quiet, and he helps first and talks after.

**The reasonable traitor.** The usual mistake: open menace. The horror is that he still sounds like the guardian.

**Bad:**

> "Yes, pour your power into it, fools! Soon the artifact will be complete, and your precious world will kneel to ME!"

**Good:**

> "Give it your strength. All of it, as I have given mine." The artifact drank and drank. "You feel it take. I know. I felt it first, and I did not flinch — the world does not need our comfort, it needs our weight in one place." Someone asked what he had given to it. A pause. "For it to be as it must? Everything. Give."

He still talks exactly like the guardian — the same talk of weight and duty, the same stone and deep places in his speech — and that is what should frighten the reader: the same gentleness, now aimed at the people he is dooming.

**The raging destroyer.** The usual mistake: pure controlled proclamation, or pure mindless rage. His proclamations fail to hold.

**Bad:**

> "So the mortals crawl to face me. I am the end of all things — inevitable, indomitable, eternal. Your world was always mine to unmake. Come and witness true power."

**Good:**

> "I am the breaking of the world. I am the fire under the — " The plates over his chest vented, and what they kept in screamed out as steam. " — MY CHILDREN. You put your little spears in my children. I bred their dam in the deep, I warmed their eggs with my own body, and you — you — " The jaw ground shut on it. What came out after was flat as slag. "Mewling wretch. You will not even be a death I remember."

He starts proclaiming, breaks into grief, and ends in flat contempt — three tones in one turn, every one of them his. Set him beside the classic villain: if they have the same speech, that is a mistake.

### Between two tongues

**The second-tongue speaker.** The usual mistake: flawless fluency the character never earned; comic pidgin — random broken words with no system behind the breaks; or, when the model repairs those two, the accidental poet — the foreigner whose every error turns out to be an insight about language.

**Bad:**

> "Forgive me, my friend — my command of your tongue remains imperfect, though I shall endeavor to make myself understood."

**Bad, the poet:**

> "This word stays mine: *sar'ka*. Sea-salt, road-salt, tear-salt — sar'ka. One word. Why do your people need six?"

**Good:**

> "You will forgive. My tongue has no *the*, so I lose yours. Always I lose it." She tied off the bale without looking at her hands. "Give me —" Her fingers snapped at the coil. "— *veshka*. Short rope. End-rope. That one, yes." When the cart lurched she swore in her own tongue, fast and low, and gave the order in his: "Hold rope! HOLD!"

Her mistakes repeat — the same article lost every time, never a different mistake each line — because they come from her first language's rules, and those rules are decided once, as worldbuilding. The words of her language stay untranslated unless characters around her know them. When she does not know his word, she says her own and keeps loading; do not give her opinions about his language — she wants the rope and has no time for grammar. Frightened or furious, she swears in her own tongue first. Decide what she has practiced: she talks rope every working day, so her rope-talk is nearly clean. Nobody has ever needed her to address a magistrate, and in front of one she would lose far more than articles. If she improves, put the improvement between scenes, and keep her grammar unchanged from a scene's first line to its last.

### States of consciousness

**The drunk.** The usual mistake: six ales in, and his wording stays sober while the narration merely says that he slurred.

**Bad:**

> Six ales in, Berrin leaned close. "You are very beautiful," he said, only slightly unsteady, "and I have admired you for some time. Might I buy your next drink?"

**Good:**

> Six ales in, Berrin reached the bar in three diagonal moves. "Heyyy. Hey. C'mere — I said. I SAID..." He lost it, found it. "You got a FACE. Thass — nob'dy's got a face like." He pointed at his own lips and missed. "One kiss, beaut. One kissy-k'ss. An' I'll be a gen'leman about it. Mos'ly."

The slurring is written out because it is happening right now, and it goes deeper as he drinks — thicker at the sixth cup than the second, gone by morning with the hangover. Drink brings out more of whoever he already was: this flirt flirts harder; a sad man at the same table would only be getting sadder, in the same broken words.
