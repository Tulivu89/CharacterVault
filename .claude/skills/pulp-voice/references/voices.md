# Voices

Read the first time two or more characters speak in a session. Condensed from the shared voice-rigor skill; its full gallery of character types is `voice-gallery.md`.

The failure: the patient sage, the doting aunt and the bratty rival all end up talking like one person, the one the model most likes to write. That person is an excellent talker: perceptive, empathetic, organized, with the next word always ready. The farmer phrases things like the archmage; the frightened child gives a tidy report; every last line is quotable; every private thought reads as the same mind.

Two tests catch it:

- **Read the dialogue without the names.** Someone who knows these characters should be able to say who spoke each line from the words, the length and what the speaker is after. A line either of two characters could have said came from neither; rewrite it in one character's words.
- **Ask what each wants from this conversation** before writing it: praise, to leave, to win, the guest comfortable, the guest gone, the subject changed. Two people who want different things can't sound the same, even about the weather. When everyone wants the scene to wrap up neatly, the model is talking to itself.

## The patterns

1. **The narrator's sentences in everyone's mouth.** Three characters share one grammar: short statement, concrete detail, flat prediction. Fix: one cuts everything short, one talks in circles and catches himself, one makes it a single aggrieved sentence because nobody asked him first.

2. **The all-knowing counselor.** A manner of speaking any character gets once a moment feels important. Its pieces arrive together: a long speech for a short question; a recital of the other character's hidden feelings and habits, all accurate ("you check the saddle straps when you think no one's watching"); feelings named and validated; exact numbers quoted for authority ("Sixty years I have kept this gate"); points numbered aloud; "I will say this once"; a small self-correction for realism; a proverb; instructions to finish ("Go now"). Any one piece can belong to one character. All of them, in everyone the story treats as important, is one person written many times.

   Bad: the doting aunt, the rival and the timid one each say "You're leaving in the morning. I've known for three days… Two things, and I'll say them once. One: the north road. Two: come back whole. Now go and sleep," differing only by an adverb and a *please*.

   Good, the same news, four people:
   > The aunt had his collar in her fingers before he finished. "In the morning? Then tonight you eat twice, and you sit still while I fix this hem, darling. No. Not a discussion." *Whoever put that look on his face, I will find them.*
   >
   > "Good. Finally some peace around here." The rival kicked a stone off the step. "Take my horse. Yours stumbles downhill, and if you die on that nag they'll say I let you." *You come back. Nobody gets to drop you but me.*
   >
   > The timid one held the clay pot out at arm's length. "It's — um. A tincture. For if. You don't have to—" She pushed it into his hand and retreated. "The morning is very soon."
   >
   > "Which road?" the old soldier asked. He heard the answer. "South bridge is out. I rode down and checked."

   Two of the same kind must differ too: two wise old women still don't sound alike. And a type is for noticing the mistake, never a ready-made way of talking to reuse.

3. **Everyone speaks equally well.** The stablehand phrases things as skillfully as the magistrate; nobody picks a wrong word or starts over. Keep at least one character in any group who talks badly, and keep them talking badly when the scene turns serious, even if their poor telling leaves something unexplained. "Cold? No. Not cold. He just — he never asked. About the barley, about mother. Thirty year. Never asked." A bandit's grammar is his own and gets worse when he's crossed, never better.

4. **Every character gets a clever line.** Somebody in a real conversation says nothing memorable all evening. One character can be the sharp one; that's a personality. The rest say what they were going to say anyway: the quartermaster counts sacks, loses count, starts over. When one character sums up another's private self, they get it wrong with full confidence, or right in words too plain to sound wise.

5. **Speech that never changes.** Drink, fear, grief and joy push a voice further in its own direction: fear makes the terse man terser and the talker unstoppable; a drunk clerk quotes statute. The flat, careful, well-spoken tone stressed characters drift into belongs to nobody. Decide for each what shows first under pressure: grammar, volume, secrets or grievance. This holds in sex scenes.

6. **Everyone answers the question.** People steer toward what they came for. In most scenes, let at least one character bargain first, stall, answer a different question, or make it about their own business. "You've asked my brother this already. I want to know why you asked him first."

7. **Subtext said out loud.** Italic thought is for what a character won't say; the gap between speech and thought is part of who they are. "Well played. Deal again." *Let him keep the first hand. The second is the one I want.* A thought never repeats the line just spoken; it goes where the line didn't. Each character keeps their own distance between the two, and thoughts get their own length, words and subjects: the schemer thinks about exits, the farmer about his knees.

8. **A quirk instead of a voice.** One surface habit and nothing else: a pet phrase every third line, an accent spelled letter by letter, a stammer on every word. Remove the quirk; if the character still sounds like themselves through what they notice, want and refuse to say, the quirk belonged to a person. A pet word used once lands harder ("Come home safe, darling."). A stammer that appears only on two subjects tells us what those subjects mean. A habit the story set up as defining (a prophet's triple echo, a knight who never says *I*) is kept, flexes with mood, and breaks once, at the moment it means most. The narrator never copies it.

## Where a voice comes from

Decide at least two of these before a character first speaks; start with what they call things and what they want.

- **What they call things and people.** The dragon's *hoard* is the merchant's *stock*, the thief's *haul*, the tax clerk's *undeclared holdings*. The same young drake is *little one* to the sage, *whelp* to the mentor, *egg of mine* to its dam.
- **What they want from this conversation.** Rarely the topic. When two characters still sound alike after a revision, change this first.
- **What they know.** A trade gives comparison imagery: a smith reaches for heat and ore, a midwife for labor and blood loss, a sailor for weather and rope.
- **What they can't say.** One subject that makes them evasive, stiff, too loud or suddenly formal. Let the scene brush against it.
- **How much control they have right now,** over the room and over themselves.
- **How much they talk.** Set it and keep it. Age doesn't inflate it. A long speech belongs to the one character who orates, at a moment made for it: a sermon, a verdict, an audience.

## Calibration

- Several counselor pieces can belong to one character when the story explains it: the quartermaster numbers his points because counting is how he thinks. The mistake starts when a second character picks them up.
- Kinship in speech is real (siblings, a regiment's slang), with a source in the story and at least one person who never picked it up.
- Terse characters stay terse; plain speech is a voice and needs no invented quirk. Some characters really are eloquent; what matters is that characters differ.
- Accent shows in vocabulary, grammar and what a character mentions, plus at most one dropped letter in a line (*'em, wha'*). Whole lines spelled phonetically are out. Misspelling is for a body state happening now (drink, a split lip, numb cold) and tracks it: two cups drops a letter, six cups loses words, morning is clean.
- A second-tongue speaker's errors follow one system from her first language, repeat the same way every time, and stay the same from a scene's first line to its last. Her practiced talk is cleaner than her rare talk. Frightened or furious, she swears in her own tongue first.
- A crowded scene needs three voices a reader can tell apart, not twelve. A two-person scene gets the full check on every line.

## Drift signals

Stop and rework the scene when:

- two lines could be reattributed without rewriting a word;
- three turns in a row share a length, a rhythm and a kind of last sentence;
- a one-line question gets a speech, or every turn grows longer as the scene goes on;
- anyone recites another character's hidden feelings, quotes exact numbers for authority, numbers points aloud, or finishes on instructions;
- a blunt, shy or silly character starts speaking in ordered paragraphs because the scene turned serious;
- nobody stumbles, misspeaks, repeats or abandons a sentence;
- a character sounds the same drunk, terrified and at ease;
- every last line is written to be quoted, or everyone answers exactly what was asked;
- a thought repeats the line just spoken, or two characters' thoughts read as one mind;
- a character is recognizable only by a pet phrase, accent or tic.
