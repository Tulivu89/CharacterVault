# Anti-patterns: what to cut, what to do instead

Read once per session, before the first story reply. §1–16 are the user's own list (pulp-voice v21); §17–26 fold in the shared slop-rigor and authorship skills where they add something §1–16 don't cover. Rewrite the beat when you hit one; a cliché's synonym is still a cliché.

**Where these apply.** Narration and italic thought. Dialogue may use a character's own trade words and habits when the story has shown that expertise or worldview: a blacksmith says *tempered*, a clerk says *deposit*, a mechanic says *autopilot*. The mechanic's plane can be on autopilot; his grief can't. A character written to talk in a banned register (the therapy-fluent modern, the corporate lifer) may talk that way, and a character who really is a machine may think like one.

All examples below are written fresh for this file, not drawn from
any copyrighted source. Use them as a template for the *kind* of fix
to make, not a phrasebook to reuse verbatim.

### 1. Stated emotion instead of physical or behavioral show

BAD: "She felt a wave of grief wash over her."
BETTER: "Her throat closed up. She had to look away from the door."

Emotion arrives as body, action, or object — not a named feeling
word plus an intensifier ("a wave of," "a pang of," "a flicker of,"
"a surge of").

### 2. Empty or abstract metaphor as scene dressing

BAD: "The city was a tapestry of contradictions, a testament to both
hope and despair."
BETTER: cut it, or land one specific concrete image doing real work:
"Gold leaf peeling off a church door two streets from a soup line."

Watch for: "tapestry," "testament to," "symphony of," "dance of,"
"echoes of," "weight of [abstract noun]," and any noun-of-noun
construction standing in for an actual image. Also feelings as buildings or physics (*tectonic, seismic, architecture, cathedral, load-bearing, gravity*), and ideas in clothing (*grief dressed as anger, the same lie in a nicer suit*). Name the thing: *he was shouting about the gate hinge because his brother was dead.*

### 3. "It wasn't just X, it was Y" and its cousins

BAD: "It wasn't just a knife fight, it was a reckoning."

Cut this construction outright — it announces meaning instead of
earning it. If a scene means something, the reader gets there from
what happens, not from being told.

Also cut on sight: "little did she know," "in that moment, she
realized," "the air was thick with tension," "what she didn't
expect was."

The same family, compressed: *X, not Y* (*cold, not cruel*), *X, never
Y* (*he asked, never begged*), and not-nouns (*the not-touching filled
the carriage*). If the meaning survives without the negated half, cut
the negated half: *Cold. He paid his men on time and never learned
their names.* Plain contrast between two real facts stays: *he wasn't
afraid of the dog; he was afraid of what his father would say about
the dog.*

### 4. Hedging, diplomatic narration

BAD: "He was, in some ways, both a hero and a villain, and perhaps
that was the point."

Pulp doesn't hedge or hold both sides up for inspection. Commit to a
character's specific contradiction through what they *do* in
different scenes — don't have the narrator editorialize about the
paradox.

Hedged description is the small version: *a sound between a laugh and
a sob*, *the corner of his mouth did something*, *not quite a smile*,
*he didn't know whether to laugh or scream*. Commit to what happened:
*she laughed once, and it broke into crying halfway.*

### 5. Sanitized stakes, consequence-free violence

BAD: characters get hit and shrug it off with zero cost; wounds
vanish between scenes; death gets announced rather than felt.

BETTER: violence leaves a body cost that lingers past the scene it
happened in — a hand that won't close right, a limp, the copper
taste, someone throwing up after. Consequences outlast their cause.

### 6. Overly reasonable, articulate dialogue

BAD: characters state their feelings and motivations plainly and
take turns being heard, like a mediated conversation.

BETTER: people talk past each other, deflect with jokes, lie,
interrupt, change the subject the second it gets close to something
true. Subtext should carry more weight than the words on the page.

### 7. Uniform sentence rhythm

BAD: a paragraph of even, medium-length, comma-balanced sentences,
each doing one polite clause of work.

BETTER: vary it hard. A three-word sentence sitting next to one that
piles clause on clause through a chase or a fight. Rhythm should
match the scene's pulse, not stay polite and evenly spaced regardless
of what's happening.

### 8. Tidy endings, therapeutic resolution

BAD: a scene wraps with a neat insight, an olive branch, or a
summarizing final line that states the takeaway for the reader.

BETTER: end on the beat that actually happened — a joke that lands
wrong, a threat left hanging, one small physical detail — not a
narrator's bow tied on top. That includes the planted quotable said "to
no one in particular" and the "...and perhaps that, in the end, was the
real victory" line: delete it; the sentence before it was the ending.

### 9. Filler intensifiers and crutch words

Cut on sight unless a specific character would actually say them out
loud: "very," "really," "seemed to," "somewhat," "a bit,"
"couldn't help but," "found himself/herself," "began to."
("began to walk" → "walked"; "couldn't help but smile" → "smiled.")

### 10. Humor that announces itself

BAD: jokes flagged by narrator commentary ("she quipped," "he joked
darkly"), or a wink to the reader that a joke just happened.

BETTER: humor lands in timing and voice, often shoved right up
against danger or violence — dry, deadpan, undercutting tension
instead of pausing to deliver a separate "funny line."

### 11. Rhetorical comparison that describes nothing (and the rule for ones that do)

BAD: "...like a ritual neither of them believed in anymore."

Sounds insightful, shows nothing. It borrows weight from a loaded
word ("ritual") instead of earning it with an actual observable
behavior. If routine is the point, show the routine — they've run
this same exchange three times this week — don't hang a poetic label
on it and call that description.

Comparisons aren't banned. They're on a very short leash:
- One per passage, max. Two in a sentence is the ceiling, and
  both must earn their spot.
- Concrete and mundane beats poetic. "The way you know a bus
  schedule" works. "Like a prayer she no longer believed in" doesn't.
- *The way* comparisons follow the same rule: concrete and mundane
  pass ("the way you know a bus schedule" is the user's own approved
  example); poetic ones fail, and so does a dash-appositive that
  elaborates the comparison into a small poem.
- Land it and get out. Never extend a comparison past its initial
  image. The second line exploring what a metaphor means is where
  it drifts into nonsense every single time. One image, then back
  to physical reality.

### 12. The tell announcing a non-tell

BAD: "His breath caught somewhere it shouldn't have, for a cut that
shallow. She noticed."
BAD: "The bandage took twice as long as it needed to. Neither of
them mentioned that."

This is the single most common failure under pressure to make a
moment feel significant: the narrator flags that something mattered
by stating a character *noticed* it, or that something wasn't
*said/mentioned/acknowledged*. It's commentary wearing subtlety's
clothes — announcing restraint instead of exercising it. If a moment
matters, let its physical consequence carry the weight (the stitches
come out crooked; the next line comes out wrong) instead of
narrating the noticing or the not-saying.

### 13. The tautological closer

BAD: "...like that was an answer, which it was."

A clause restates its own point for a rhythmic click and adds no new
information. If a gesture already reads as an answer, the sentence
doesn't need to confirm that it read as one.

These three compound badly with each other, and they get *worse*,
not better, the more a scene is trying to matter — romantic tension,
grief, a big reveal. The instinct to sound literary under pressure is
exactly where ornament substitutes for specificity. When a scene
feels important, that's the moment to get plainer and more physical,
not more rhetorical.

### 14. Narrator pontificating about absent actors

BAD: "...which was either calculated or the result of whoever tied
it not measuring."
BAD: "...meant someone with rank wanted her alive, which meant
someone with rank was either sentimental or had plans, and in his
experience the plans were always worse than the sentiment."

The narrator fills descriptive space by running inferential chains
about unnamed people who aren't in the scene — speculating about
their motivations, their intent, their reasoning — instead of
describing what is physically present. It reads as worldbuilding
cleverness but it's filler: the reader gets a narrator thinking out
loud about off-screen decisions when they should be getting the mud,
the post, the rope length. If the rope is too short to lie down,
show her unable to lie down. Don't explain *why* it's that length
via a speculation about a person who isn't here.

This is especially common in scene-setting, where the reflex is to
make the environment feel considered by attributing design intent to
it rather than just showing what's there.

### 15. "Shouldn't have X but did" — defying-expectations filler

BAD: "...short legs that shouldn't have carried the weight but did."
BAD: "A voice that had no right being that soft."
BAD: "A smile that shouldn't have worked but did."

A cousin of #3. The narrator sets up an expectation, immediately
contradicts it, and presents the contradiction as characterization.
It does nothing — the reader hasn't had time to form the expectation
being defied, so the defiance is empty. Just describe the legs, the
voice, the smile. If they're surprising, the reader will register
the surprise from the description itself.

### 16. "Not X. Y." — the dramatic correction

BAD: "Not fists. Claws."
BAD: "Not a cry. A command."
BAD: "Not anger. Something worse."

The staccato negation-then-correction, delivered in fragments for
rhythm. It's a reveal structure applied to something that isn't a
reveal — the reader doesn't need to be told what something *wasn't*
in order to understand what it *is*. "Her fingers clawed into the
mud" does the work without the theatrical two-step. Occasional use
in dialogue is fine (people actually talk this way sometimes); in
narration it's almost always the narrator performing a beat that the
image should be doing on its own.

### 17. Hypophora

BAD: "Was he afraid? Of course he was afraid."
BETTER: "He was afraid."

Don't ask a question in narration and answer it yourself.

### 18. Recycled tension tells

BAD: jaw tightening, a muscle in the forearm, knuckles whitening, molars grinding, scene after scene.

Put tension in behavior: he set the cup down with too much care; he answered a question she hadn't asked; he got very polite. When it does go in the body, vary the body: breathing, hands gone still, a voice dropping.

### 19. Nominalization echo

BAD: "He was right, and the rightness of it followed her home." "She knew, and the knowing was its own answer."

A quality turned into a noun and commented on repeats the first clause. "He was right, and she couldn't stop thinking about it." Or just "She knew."

### 20. Word-echo and the triple

BAD: "He said nothing. The nothing filled the room." / "No letter came. No rider came. No word came."

A word carried across a period for cadence, or three parallel clauses where one carries the meaning. "Three weeks, and nothing had come." Repetition that follows a real sequence stays, and so does a character's own screamed repetition in dialogue.

### 21. Abstractions doing people's verbs

BAD: "The silence answered him." "Grief arrived a week late." "The room went quiet."

Give the verb to a person or a body: "Nobody answered him." "A week after the funeral she started crying in the pantry with the door shut." "Everyone stopped talking."

### 22. Minds as math, machines and software

BAD: *the arithmetic of it, calculus, processing, recalibrating, clockwork, autopilot, wired, glitch, buffering, bandwidth* for thought or feeling.

"She thought of the rent, then her sister, then the rent again." "He still set out two cups every morning." (The venue rule above covers trade talk and real machines.)

### 23. Consequence as commerce

BAD: "The stopping was paid for in the shake of his arm." "She had earned the silence that followed."

"His arm shook the whole way down." "When she finished, nobody argued." Money words for money stay: the ledger, the toll, the debt, a sentence measured in a currency.

### 24. The fantasy vague-out

BAD: "glittering riches beyond counting, treasures of ages heaped in the dark, and the air itself hummed with ancient power."

The more invented the thing, the more real pieces it needs: name materials, colors, counts and wear a reader could point at. "Square-holed brass from the old river mints, mixed with silver gone black." A place the scene acts in gets built; a corridor passed through gets one anchor detail. Wrongness is recognizable parts arranged wrong.

### 25. Word list

- *devastating* for anyone attractive → what the attractiveness does: the men by the door forgot what they were saying.
- *wound* as a metaphor for a person, memory or place → name it: the year he won't talk about. Literal wounds and *old wounds, wounded pride* pass.
- *shape* for things without one (*the shape of his grief, something in the shape of an apology*) → what was there: "I shouldn't have," and nothing after it.
- heartbeats and breaths as clocks (*a heartbeat late, for the space of three breaths, a beat too long*) → late, brief, or a real measure: before she finished the sentence.
- *this particular, that certain* → cut. *just short of* → what happened. *a stretch* of time, *dead air* → the hours, the quiet. *rendered* for prose or feeling → written, shown.
- Bowdlerized blanks → write the word, the slur too if the character would say it.

### 26. Default numbers and names

The model's "random" picks read as machine-made: three of everything, seven, nine, twelve, forty-seven; 3:14 a.m., 11:47, "seventeen minutes"; ages 27, 29, 32, 34, 42. Take numbers from the world (a shift ends when the shift ends; the fare is the fare), use uneven values, or roll one: `python3 -c "import random; print(random.randint(12, 90))"`, and keep the result even when it feels odd. Characters round in character: "a couple hundred", "pushing sixty". If a number doesn't matter, leave it out.

Names come from place, era and class (the profile may set a naming rule; follow it). Skip the defaults: Elara, Kael, Lyra, Thorne, Seraphina, Silas, Vex, Nyx; Sarah Chen, Marcus, Elena, Maya, Ethan, Mira. Vary syllable counts and first letters across a cast, and allow plain or ugly names.
