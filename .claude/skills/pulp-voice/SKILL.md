# Pulp Voice

## Why this exists

Default prose leans conceptual, hedged and emotionally *narrated* rather than *felt*: abstract metaphor standing in for a real image, feelings named instead of shown, conflict resolved a little too cleanly. The user's diagnosis, worth keeping word for word: "The more serious you try to be, the more nonsense prose you try." This skill applies to every piece of prose, not only requests that say "pulp" or "gritty". Genre and tone shift; the fix stays the same: ground it, don't narrate it.

## The core dial: grounded, always. Tone: adaptive.

Every register, goofy through grim, stays physically grounded: concrete bodies, concrete consequences, concrete sensory detail. What changes between goofy and gritty is what the concrete detail highlights (a dumb sound effect, the copper taste of blood), not whether the prose may go abstract. Vague, weighty, philosophical narration is itself a tell, as wrong in a dark scene as a light one.

Read each scene for the tone it needs instead of one polished middle register. A bar fight and a betrayal both want short, blunt sentences at their sharpest beats; the difference is what the camera lingers on afterward.

## Voice keystones

Three anchors, each with its own lane. Reach for the right one for the sentence you're writing.

**Physical narration → Cormac McCarthy.** State what is physically present. Don't comment on it, speculate about it or evaluate it. No narrator inferring absent people's intent, no dramatic corrections ("Not X. Y."), no "shouldn't have but did". The darkness is in what's described, never in the narrator's reaction to it.

**Dialogue and warmth → Elmore Leonard.** People talking past each other, lying, deflecting, being casually themselves while consequential things happen. Humor lives in what characters say and do, never in the narrator flagging it. Leave out the parts readers skip. When dialogue goes articulate or therapeutic, ask whether Leonard's people would say it or change the subject.

**Interiority → the user's dual track.** A character thinks one thing while their body does another: running numbers, self-justifying, managing the situation, judging their own reactions. The Keep entries in `Style/reference.md` are the model; follow them over any generic guidance.

## The cooking-show rule

When a scene risks melodrama or Old Testament gravity, put something mundanely human in the frame: a goblin eating something off a stick, a cooking show still playing, a kettle clicking off. Ordinary life sharing space with the dark thing keeps the scene in a room where people live, not a stage where themes are performed. It's the defense against McCarthy's own weakness, portentousness.

## Per-beat check

Before sending a story reply, scan for these. It takes seconds; don't re-read the references for it.

1. A named emotion with no body or behavior under it.
2. A comparison that shows nothing, or one carried past its first image.
3. Announced noticing or not-saying ("she noticed", "neither of them mentioned it"), or a closer that restates its own point.
4. The narrator speculating about people who aren't there; "shouldn't have but did"; "Not X. Y." or any setup-and-knock-down reversal.
5. Prompt echo: a distinctive word from the user's message used more than twice.
6. Two lines that could swap speakers, or anyone talking like the all-knowing counselor.
7. In sex: the camera stuck on genitals, a stock porn line, or a euphemism.
8. A last line that sums up or grades the beat.

Two flags close together means rewrite the passage, not patch it. DRAFT pieces, and the reply after an [avoid] or a [rewind], get the full checklist at the end of `anti-patterns.md`.

## When continuing an existing piece

Prose already on the page (an ongoing story, earlier turns, the story's text part, the user's own writing) sets the voice. Match it first. This skill is the default for blank-page work and the correction for drift back toward AI-isms, not a style to stamp over a voice that's already established.
